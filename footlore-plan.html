Footlore

A pocket tour guide that reads the ground you're standing on. It knows the story of the building across the street and the best plate of food within a ten-minute walk — and it can stitch both into an afternoon.

The bet

Search apps answer questions. A guide volunteers things.

Maps and review apps are excellent when you already know what you're looking for. They're useless at the thing a good human guide does constantly: noticing that the unremarkable arch you just walked past is 700 years old, and that the place doing the best thing on this street has no queue right now.

Footlore's job is to be worth opening when you have no query. That sets three hard product rules: it must say something interesting within two seconds of launch, it must never show a wall of undifferentiated pins, and every item has to justify itself in one sentence a person would actually repeat out loud.

Nearby feed

The default launch state. A ranked, mixed feed of sights and food within a walkable radius, each with a one-line reason. For the ten minutes between things.

Time to spare

Guided walk

Four to six stops routed into a loop that lands you at a good dinner. Narration triggers on arrival; skipping a stop reroutes the rest.

Getting oriented

Map view

For the "what's over there?" question. Pins carry meaning — sights and food are visually distinct, and low-signal noise is filtered out entirely.

Ranking

What makes something worth interrupting you for

A single score decides feed order for both sights and food, so the two can share one list. Weights differ by category — a landmark doesn't need a rating, a restaurant does.

Walk time30%
Story strength25%
Rating & volume20%
Open right now15%
Not seen before10%

"Story strength" is the interesting one and the part that has to be built, not bought: a heritage listing, a long Wikipedia article, or a dense OSM tag set all suggest there's something to say. Everything below a floor gets dropped rather than shown weakly — an empty feed with three great things beats a full one with twenty bus stops.

Data

Free where history lives, paid only where ratings matter

The hybrid split keeps per-user cost near zero for the sightseeing half, which is the half people open the app for.

The one architectural decision that matters: the client never calls a provider directly. A thin backend does the fan-out, joins OSM to Wikipedia to Places, caches by geohash tile, and returns one clean Place shape. That keeps the keys off the device, makes the paid calls controllable, and means the SwiftUI app later is a new front end rather than a second integration.

Known risks

What could sink it

Rural emptiness

OSM density collapses outside cities. Needs an honest empty state that widens the radius and says so, rather than padding the feed with petrol stations.

Narration that reads as filler

Generated blurbs with nothing to work from are the fastest way to lose trust. Hard rule: no source material above the story-strength floor, no card.

Places cost curve

Cheap for one user, alarming at scale if every pan and filter triggers a request. Tile-and-cache at the backend, and only request food fields on demand.

Battery and background

Continuous GPS on a two-hour walk is brutal. Coarse updates plus region monitoring at stop boundaries, never a live high-accuracy stream.
